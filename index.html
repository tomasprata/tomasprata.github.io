AI Earnings-Release Return Forecaster

Ranking how every stock reacts to its earnings release

A stock price already contains everything the market knows; it only moves when something new arrives. I built a Python system around that idea: Claude isolates the new information in each earnings release, a linear model on six variables, fitted on 2,577 past events, turns it into a predicted next-day return, a Student-t distribution fitted by maximum likelihood converts that return into a percentile, and a blend with two prompt-engineered LLM predictors cuts the noise. It ran live as a webhook on 1,237 earnings events.

Technical details

The idea

If a fact was already known, it is already in the price. Revenue figures were forecast, growth rates modelled, acquisitions announced months earlier. The extracted parameters are therefore not a summary of the release — they are a filter that discards everything already known and keeps only what is new today.

Stage 1 — Claude reads, Python calculates

Claude reads each release as numbered bullets, given only the consensus EPS and expected net income — no ticker, date or quarter, so it cannot recognise the company or recall the outcome. It returns a JSON ledger validated against a schema: for every new fact, the bullet it came from, the parameter (past results, guidance, payout, tone), the type (an actual/expected pair, a percentage, or a qualitative count) and whether lower is better for that metric.

The division of labour is strict: the model classifies, Python computes. The model decides a metric’s polarity (a reading task); Python owns the sign and every subtraction (an arithmetic task). Every number Claude reports must exist in the bullet it cites or the extraction is rejected; compared figures must cover the same period; each fact counts once. Each event is extracted in several independent runs and median-aggregated. Measurable gaps are then mapped onto a fixed ladder:

extract.py — a 2% beat scores +1, a 20% miss scores −6
LADDER = (1.0, 3.0, 6.0, 9.0, 12.0, 15.0)     # % boundaries

def band(pct: float) -> int:
    """Constant-interval ladder, capped at +/-6."""
    a = abs(pct)
    level = 6
    for i, edge in enumerate(LADDER):
        if a <= edge:
            level = i
            break
    return int(math.copysign(level, pct)) if level else 0

Stage 3 — from return to percentile: MLE and the Student-t

Earnings-day returns are fat-tailed. Fitting Normal, Student-t and Johnson-SU distributions by maximum likelihood to 6,858 earnings-day returns, the Normal collapses in the tails while the t tracks the data (pooled df ≈ 1.8; per-quarter fits 2.4–3.4). The degrees of freedom are fixed at 3 and the prediction is mapped through the closed-form t(3) CDF:

percentile = Ft(3)( (r̂ − c) ⁄ s ),   s = 11.92% ⁄ √3 = 0.0688   (Var = 3s² at df = 3)
ret_dist.py — no numerical stack needed in the live container
def t3_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """CDF of a standard Student's t with df = 3, in closed form.
    F(x) = 1/2 + (1/pi) * [ arctan(x/sqrt3) + (x/sqrt3)/(1 + x^2/3) ]
    Verified against scipy.stats.t.cdf to 1e-12."""
    u = x / math.sqrt(3.0)
    return 0.5 + (math.atan(u) + u / (1.0 + u * u)) / math.pi

The S-curve damps extreme predictions, which are the least reliable: submitting Ft(3) scored 0.0322 against 0.0296 for a linear rescaling (+9%). Because Var = 3s² at df = 3, the scale is s = sd/√3 (using sd directly would make the curve 1.73× too wide), and a median absolute deviation converts to scale with the t(3) factor 0.7649 rather than the Normal’s 1.4826.

Rules, leakage and deployment

The contest banned market data observed after each event’s knowledge cutoff. The market term was re-keyed to the last close at or before the cutoff (it had sat one session late on 181 events, 2.76%), and the adaptive scale — fed by realised returns of earlier reporters — was switched off; neither change reduced accuracy (blend ΔR² 0.0791 → 0.0794). The pipeline ran live as a serverless webhook on Modal, and predict.py --verify re-derives every constant from the training events and checks that they match.

Live results

On the 1,237 live Q3 2026 events: R² 0.154, double the earnings surprise alone; next-day direction right 62% of the time, 73% on moves of 20% or more; 85% of the top-10% performers predicted to rise. Average next-day return ran from −8.4% for the most bearish fifth of predictions to +5.2% for the most bullish — a 13.6 percentage-point spread. Ranked 55th of 204.

Is the US in an Abundant, Ample, or Scarce Reserve Environment?

Estimating the nonlinear demand curve for US bank reserves

Whether the US banking system holds abundant, ample or scarce reserves determines how much liquidity the Fed can drain before money-market rates react. I reconstructed a daily reserve series from the Fed’s balance sheet, regressed the EFFR − IORB spread on reserves with controls, and showed that the apparent negative relationship over the year is spurious — driven by structural breaks — so the US is in an abundant reserve environment.

Technical details

Framework

In the standard model of the fed funds market, the equilibrium rate is set by the demand for and supply of reserves. The regimes are defined by the slope of demand: no response of rates to reserves (abundant), a mild negative response (ample), or a steep one (scarce) — together with where the EFFR sits relative to the interest on reserve balances (IORB).

Central bank balance-sheet analysis — building a daily reserve series

Reserves are only published weekly, which hides the day-to-day supply shocks that move money markets. I reconstructed a daily series from the items of the Fed’s balance sheet that are reported daily, spreading weekly changes in securities held outright evenly through the week:

R̂T = RT−1 − ΔTGAT + ΔRepoT − ΔReverse RepoT + ΔSecurities held outrightT

The estimate tracks the official weekly reserves closely over January 2021 – January 2026 and is then used only to fill the gaps between weekly prints. Its 20 largest deviations from the weekly figures all trace back to identifiable flows: 7 tax dates, 6 Treasury issuance/settlement dates and 7 government-spending dates — real supply shifts, not noise.

Specification

The dependent variable is the EFFR − IORB spread, which removes changes in the administered rate (the IORB is the floor, enforced by arbitrage). Reserves enter as a reciprocal to capture the convex demand curve — a positive β1 means rates fall as reserves rise:

EFFR − IORB = β0 + β1 · (1 ⁄ Reserves) + β2 · Bank assets + β3 · NFCI + β4 · Expectations + Σ βj · Calendar dummyj

Taken at face value, the one-year result points to an ample regime: at $3.1 trillion of reserves, an average daily swing of $40bn moves the spread by about 0.4bp, and roughly $105bn — less than one standard deviation of reserves ($206bn) — moves it by 1bp.

The puzzle — a spurious relationship

The EFFR − IORB spread has three structural periods: flat before 22 September 2025, variable until 26 November 2025, flat again after. Every regression that spans a break shows a strong negative relationship; every regression inside a single period shows none. Over the year the Fed was slowly shrinking reserves while the spread happened to step up — a coincidental trend. Since the EFFR reacts to reserves day to day, and there is no day-to-day relationship within any stable period, the full-sample correlation is spurious.

Conclusion

Within each structural period reserves have no effect on the spread, and the EFFR trades below the IORB: the US has been in an abundant reserve environment over the past year.

EnerSys (NYSE: ENS) — Lead-Battery Cash-Flow Model

My part of the sell pitch: how much cash EnerSys’s lead batteries will generate through 2030

EnerSys was re-rated as an AI data-center winner. In a five-person pitch, my part was the lead-battery business of its Network & Infrastructure Solutions (NIS) segment. I broke it into a volume model and a gross-margin model, then combined them into the change in lead-battery gross profit to 2030 under bear, normal and bull scenarios. The result fed the team’s DCF, which valued the stock at $127.63 against a $159.95 price; the fund approved the sell as a 4% position.

How I broke it down

Volume model

Each end market and region is compounded on its own, then weighted by region and by end market. Market growth is held constant; EnerSys’s share change starts from a 2026 estimate and fades each year, because share gains revert:

Index(e, r, t) = 100 · Πyears ≤ t (1 + gmarket(e, r)) · (1 + gshare(e, r, year)),   gshare(t) = gshare(2026) · (1 − λ)t − 2026

Data center (30%). Management guided US data-center volume to +20% for FY26, so the 2026 share gain is backed out of the market growth: gshare = 1.20 ÷ (1 + gmarket) − 1 (+17.1% in the normal case). Data center is more competitive than telecom, so share fades faster (λ = 40%). Europe and Asia are calibrated from the US: +17.1% ÷ 5 positive competitive drivers = +3.41% per net driver; Europe nets +1, Asia −2.

Telecom (50%). Normal market growth of −4% (US), −5.5% (Europe) and +3.5% (Asia) as lithium substitutes for lead, with share changes fading at λ = 30%. The US weight (75–85%) is reconciled to the company’s 72% Americas share of sales.

Utility (20%) and the end-market weights. Utility is held flat. The weights are never disclosed, so I inferred them from FY24, when Energy Systems volume fell 13% on a telecom capex pause: −13% = 0.18 × (+10%) + wtel × gtel, which gives 30 / 50 / 20 for a −25% telecom decline.

Validation. Rebuilding the FY26 quarterly revenue bridge gives volume +2.9%, price/mix +3.3% and FX +1.8%, against +3%, +3% and +2% in the 10-K.

Gross-margin model

EnerSys’s reported product margin mixes in lithium, power electronics and chargers, so lead margins are built from peers whose sales are ~100% lead batteries. Six peers were screened and three kept: Clarios for US/Europe (19.3%; range 17.3–21.4%) and Sebang and Leoch for Asia (13.5%; range 8.9–16.6%). The range then widens in three layers:

Lower(t) = Base − √( dlow² + t · σ² ),   σ = 1.44 pts a year (Sebang’s year-over-year changes)

On top of the peer base comes an EnerSys premium from 2026 (+1.5 pts US/Europe, +0.75 pts Asia: industrial cells, No. 1–2 share, TPPL technology), then yearly factor adjustments — competition, utilisation (≈ 0.22 pts of margin per 1% of volume), non-lead input costs and EU regulation — each with ±50% uncertainty that accumulates over time. The regions are blended at today’s revenue mix: 0.9435 × US/Europe + 0.0565 × Asia.

Technical details

A Python engine that writes the models

The workbooks are generated by a Python (openpyxl) engine with a strict design contract: only input cells hold typed values; every other cell is written as an Excel formula, never a computed number. The cash-flow statement is derived — every non-cash balance-sheet row is consumed exactly once by a cash-flow line — so Assets − Liabilities − Equity = 0 is an algebraic identity rather than a plug, and the engine refuses to emit a workbook where it fails. Interest runs off beginning-of-period balances, so there are no circular references and every recalculation is deterministic — the property Monte Carlo needs.

Nine tabs per company (drivers, assumptions, IS, BS, CF, schedules, DCF, checks), FY2021–25 actual and FY2026–30 forecast. Revenue is built bottom-up — SAP as worldwide software spending × SAP’s share; Visa as global card payment volume × Visa’s share × back-solved take rates — and historical ratios are back-solved from filings.

Mean-variance optimisation — the math

Expected returns are each stock’s CAPM cost of equity from its DCF model, and the covariance matrix Σ comes from five years of weekly total returns to 7 September 2026, in local currency, annualised. The optimiser solves for the long-only portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio, measuring each name’s excess return over its own risk-free rate (EUR 3.40% for SAP):

maxw   Σi wi (μi − rf,i) ⁄ √(wᵀ Σ w)    s.t.   Σi wi = 1,   wi ≥ 0,    μi = rf,i + βi · ERP

Stability. Re-run on six estimation windows (weekly, monthly and daily data; 5- and 10-year lookbacks; EUR or USD base), SAP’s weight ranges from 0% to 17% and drops out under both monthly windows — mean-variance weights are highly sensitive to the covariance estimate.

Economic Derivations

Proving core results in monetary theory — from the micro-foundations of money to the Phillips curve

I do not consider a result understood until I can derive it. My education has been built around proofs: starting from primitives — preferences, technologies, the rules of a market — and working step by step to the conclusion, so that every assumption is visible and I can see exactly which one drives the result and what breaks when it changes. These derivations from Monetary Theory and Policy apply that approach to three foundations of monetary economics. First, a Kiyotaki–Wright search model with a cost of holding money, solved for the threshold at which fiat money is accepted and the conditions under which a monetary equilibrium exists. Second, the demand for central bank reserves, used to derive how a central bank moves the overnight rate when reserves are scarce and why, once they are abundant, only the administered rates matter. Third, the expectations-augmented Phillips curve, built from CES demand, markup pricing and a share of sticky-price firms.

1 · Money as a Medium of Exchange with a Holding Cost (Kiyotaki–Wright)

Why does anyone accept intrinsically worthless paper money in exchange for real goods? The Kiyotaki–Wright search model answers this from first principles: agents meet at random, trade only when both sides gain, and money has value only because others are expected to accept it. Here I add a cost of holding money, ξ\xi (a storage cost or an inflation tax), and derive how it changes whether money circulates at all.

Setup

A fraction M∈(0,1)M\in(0,1) of agents holds one unit of fiat money and 1−M1-M holds one good. Any agent likes a fraction x∈(0,1)x\in(0,1) of goods; consumption yields utility U>0U>0, after which the agent produces a new good. Agents meet at random, one meeting per period, and discount at rate rr. Let π\pi be the probability that an agent accepts money and Π\Pi the probability that other agents accept money. An agent who ends a period holding money pays a utility cost ξ∈(0,∞)\xi\in(0,\infty).

Deriving the value of holding money and of holding a good

The value of each state is written recursively, as a Bellman equation: today’s payoff plus the discounted value of the state the agent ends up in, weighted by the probability of each kind of meeting.

Money holder.

If he trades he consumes (UU) and becomes a commodity holder; if not, he keeps the money and pays ξ\xi: VM,t=11+r[(VC,t+1+U)(1−M)xΠ+(VM,t+1−ξ)(1−(1−M)xΠ)]\boxed{\,V_{M,t}=\frac{1}{1+r}\Big[\big(V_{C,t+1}+U\big)(1-M)x\Pi+\big(V_{M,t+1}-\xi\big)\big(1-(1-M)x\Pi\big)\Big]\,}

Deriving the optimal acceptance strategy and the acceptance threshold Π*\Pi^*

A commodity holder accepts money only if holding money is worth more than holding a good. Imposing stationarity and symmetry turns this comparison into a single threshold Π*\Pi^* on how widely money is accepted by everyone else. 2 · Reserve Demand and the Policy Rate: Corridor and Floor Systems

Central banks steer the overnight interest rate by managing the supply of bank reserves against a downward-sloping demand curve, bounded by two administered rates: the lending rate iLi_L (the ceiling) and the rate paid on reserves idi_d (the floor). These derivations work through how policy operates in each regime — when reserves are scarce and the rate moves along the curve, and when reserves are abundant and the rate sits on the floor, the shift the Federal Reserve and the ECB made after 2008.

Setup

The transmission mechanism.

In period 0 the equilibrium at i=4.50%i=4.50\% has a reserve supply of $3.23 trillion; in period 1 the equilibrium at i=4.25%i=4.25\% has $3.46 trillion. Reserves are the currency held by banks plus their deposits at the central bank, and banks hold reserves in proportion to the assets they hold.

When the central bank conducts an open-market purchase, it buys securities from the market and raises the money holdings of households, corporations and banks. Securities bought from banks raise their reserves directly; even if banks use part of them to buy assets, required reserves rise with total assets. The extra money of households and firms is deposited in banks, adding further to banks’ cash, which they can invest in securities or deposit at the central bank.

With more reserves than they need, banks are more willing to lend them out overnight. The larger supply of overnight loans puts downward pressure on the overnight rate, moving the equilibrium down the demand curve. This is the pre-financial-crisis regime: there are no excess reserves, the period-0 rate is above the deposit rate, and the operation moves the equilibrium rate.

Why open-market operations stop working when reserves are abundant, and how the rate is set instead

Once reserves are abundant the demand curve is flat at the deposit rate, so the quantity of reserves no longer moves the price; the rate is set by the administered rates instead.

The floor.

If the period-0 rate already sits at the deposit rate (R≥RkR\ge R_k), an open-market purchase that would normally push the overnight rate down has no effect on the equilibrium rate. No bank lends overnight to another bank at a lower rate than it earns by depositing at the central bank: why lend to a counterparty with almost zero risk at a lower rate when one can lend to a counterparty with zero risk at a higher rate?

Moreover, a rate below idi_d would be an arbitrage: banks would borrow in the overnight market and deposit at the central bank. Exploiting it raises the demand for overnight funds and pushes the rate back up to idi_d.

In practice, overnight rates in both the US and the euro area trade slightly below the deposit rate. The overnight market also includes shadow banks and other non-bank entities that cannot deposit at the central bank; since they cannot exploit the arbitrage, only banks apply upward pressure on the market rate. The persistence of this gap suggests that non-bank demand for liquidity is strong enough to absorb all the supply banks provide. It may also be a deliberate design: leaving banks a risk-free margin allows them to narrow their lending spreads, supporting transmission to the real economy through mortgage rates and business borrowing.

Moving the rate with the administered rate.

From the demand curve, i=idi=i_d whenever R≥6R\ge 6. Since the current equilibrium has i=id=1.5%i=i_d=1.5\%, any change in idi_d changes ii one-for-one as long as R≥6R\ge 6: id′=1.50%−0.25%=1.25%⇒i′=1.25%i_d' = 1.50\%-0.25\% = 1.25\% \quad\Longrightarrow\quad i' = \boxed{\,1.25\%\,}

3 · Monopolistic Competition, Natural Output and the Phillips Curve

The Phillips curve links inflation to the gap between output and its natural level. Rather than assuming it, this section builds it from micro-foundations: households choose consumption and labour, firms with market power set prices as a markup over marginal cost, and a share of firms set their prices in advance. The result is the expectations-augmented Phillips curve, the backbone of New Keynesian monetary policy analysis.

Setup

  • πt=πte\pi_t=\pi_t^e: output is at its natural level, because firms and households set production, consumption, labour supply and prices at their optimal levels.

  • πt>πte\pi_t>\pi_t^e: wages, which are contracted on expected inflation, and sticky prices were set too low. Real costs are lower than expected, encouraging firms to expand production; households, meanwhile, consume more than their optimal amount. Output rises above its natural level.

Are People in Japan Storing Their Money in Mattresses?

How three decades of Japanese monetary policy shook global markets in August 2024

An explainer on the Bank of Japan, written for a general audience. It starts from the August 2024 sell-off — the S&P 500 fell 7% in days as the BoJ raised rates and the yen appreciated — and works back through the yen carry trade, Japan’s deflationary 1990s, the world’s first zero-rate policy in 2001, negative rates and yield-curve control in 2016, and the recent exit.

Monetary policyBank of JapanYield-curve controlCarry tradeEconomic writing
Technical details

The trigger

When the BoJ raised rates, the yen appreciated and investors who had borrowed in yen faced sharply higher repayments in dollar terms, forcing sales of securities and a scramble for liquidity.

Why borrow in yen

Two decades of near-zero rates made Japan the cheapest place in the world to borrow; Japanese banks became the largest cross-border lenders, with about $4.8 trillion of claims abroad.

The policy experiment

Traces the asset-bubble collapse and deflation of the 1990s, zero rates from 2001, negative short rates with yield-curve control from 2016 — and how the BoJ steered both short and long rates through asset purchases.

Did it work?

People did not move their money into mattresses, but growth and inflation stayed weak and bank margins suffered. With the BoJ now above zero and YCC abandoned, inflation and wages are finally rising.
